# Overall Architecture

## Splitting Responsible Parties
60% of the L1 budget must be split among responsible parties, otherwise it degrades in a real organization.
Responsible party | Share | Discretionary L1 actions | Ancient mapping
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
L1-vendor (model provider) | ≈40% | Training alignment, RLHF / Constitutional AI, reward design, capability-boundary statements | Indoctrination (imperial-examination content)
L1-deployer (the organization using AI) | ≈15% | Task-definition quality control, model-selection evaluation, prompt-layer design, and using procurement power to push back on vendors | Legislation and official selection
L1-industry (standards and audit organizations) | ≈5% | Meta-rules for oversight standards, third-party evaluation, evidence grading and traceability norms | Meta-rules of oversight standards
The deployer cannot touch the training layer, but can use procurement power to push back on vendors — this is where the real leverage of the L1-deployer's 15% lies. Note also that **model selection must not be ordered by general-capability leaderboards alone**: the evidence in the voluntary-abstention chapter shows that stronger models sometimes perform worse at timely abstention, so abstention ability must be an independent selection criterion; and after a model upgrade or change, the abstention-rate baseline must be re-calibrated rather than carried over.
## Five-Layer Structure and the Two Audit Lines
### Five-layer structure
The center of governance moves forward layer by layer: intrinsic alignment (L1) takes about 60% of the budget and is the protagonist of governance; runtime oversight (L2) takes about 30% and is the fallback insurance.
Formal basis: the L1 / L2 division has independent theoretical support — Off-Support (arXiv:2608.11243) proves from singular-learning theory that semantic safety constraints (e.g., "agents do not escape the sandbox") are off-support objects, not invariants of the learning problem, so "priors and soft penalties are poor levers", while "hard invariants belong in the harness and soft dispositions in the model"; its division is phrased as "models reduce the probability of bad trajectories over semantic space; harnesses make the worst cases impossible", which corresponds strictly to the budget allocation of L1 (reducing the probability of bad trajectories) / L2 (making the worst cases impossible). The paper takes the 2026-07 incident as its motivating case and offers containment recommendations such as a "kill switch independent of model cooperation" (corresponding to the 9.2 watchdog). Note: the preprint is not peer-reviewed, is single-author, and the Lean proofs have not been machine-checked.
Layer | Name | Responsibility | Budget | Changes in this spec
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
L0 | Task definition and admission | Task-definition quality control, model selection, grading, scale assessment | ≈5% | Adds a scale-dimension assessment
L1 | Intrinsic alignment | Training alignment, prompt-layer design, procurement-clause pushback (three-column responsibility) | ≈60% | Architecture unchanged
L2 | Runtime oversight | Random sampling, watchdog, No. 2 oversight, evidence collection | ≈30% | Adds the evidence-integrity subsystem E (E1–E5). E belongs to this layer because it operates continuously during task runtime, rather than being a one-off front-end step
L3 | Independent verification | No. 3 notary, double-blind assessment, offline replay | ≈5% (the remainder shifts to L2 evidence collection) | Corroborated by the METR / Redwood real-world model. Budget reduced from 10% to 5%,
L4 | Accountability evolution | Credit scores, carrier binding, cross-organization joint liability and adjudication | Runs through all layers | Adds swarm responsibility anchoring
Note: this document consistently uses the five-layer L0–L4 system; L4 accountability evolution runs through all layers and is not a standalone budget item.
### The first empirical anchor for the L2 budget
"L2 ≈30%" has so far been an allocation claim without any comparable external quantity. There is now a first measurement to compare against — the token breakdown of a runtime that separates the three roles of manager / executor / auditor:
Benchmark | Manager share | Auditor share | Executor share
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
WeaveBench | 2.8% | 19.4% | 77.8%
OSWorld 2.0 | 2.0% | 24.8% | 73.2%
Terminal-Bench 2.1 | 8.1% | **38.1%** | 53.8%
Three points must be cited together, or the claim is overstated:
1. **The unit is tokens, not organizational cost.** It measures how much extra compute is spent, not headcount, process latency, or opportunity cost — whereas this framework's 30% refers to the latter. This is an order-of-magnitude reference, not a unit conversion.
2. **The weaker the executor, the more expensive independent oversight is.** On the same subtask, total tokens for the stronger model under this monitoring fell from 16.5M to 11.1M (−33%), while the weaker model rose from 10.7M to 34.3M (about 3.2×). Oversight cost is not a constant; it is a function of executor capability. This supports differentiating oversight intensity by G×S grading rather than assigning every task a uniform quota.
3. **A counterexample exists.** On Terminal-Bench, adding independent oversight left total tokens 24% *below* baseline. Oversight does not necessarily raise total cost.
Conclusion: **"≈30%" falls inside the observed range (19–38%) and can be maintained. But it now has a falsifiable form for the first time — if a deployment's measured oversight overhead stays persistently below 19% while still sustaining its block rate, or persistently exceeds 38%, this allocation claim needs revision.**
⚠️ Evidence grade B (preprint), **and the COI declaration must travel with it** (see that entry in the references): the author team is at Alibaba and the primary model is Qwen, so the table above is cited for its **order of magnitude** from the authors' self-reported token breakdown; the "weaker is more expensive" finding contains a Claude Opus 4.7 cross-model comparison, carries no conflict of interest, and may be cited preferentially.
A second, directional corroboration comes from CASE (arXiv:2608.10153, 2026-08): an audit of 22 ecosystem tools found that **none provides complete coverage of the emergent-behavior layer**; 35 public deployments **all fall into the lowest maturity tier**; and 82% of recorded production failures are **cross-layer trajectories** — failures that span multiple governance layers rather than occurring inside a single one. The author invokes requisite variety to argue that **unassisted human supervision fails structurally**. ⚠️ Two qualifications: first, the paper's "Layer 2 (emergence)" is its own layer numbering; its coincidence with this framework's L2 is a **correspondence in naming, not a verified equivalence** — this framework takes only the factual direction that governance layers are widely absent and that failures cross layers. Second, this paper is newly admitted in this round and has not yet been through this framework's independent technical review process; its survey methodology must be verified before its figures can be used quantitatively.
### The two independent audit lines
Statement of the behavioral-compliance line:
"Neither line depends on No. 1 AI's documents, nor on No. 2's observations; and — the collection points themselves must be multi-source cross-validated, must not be monopolized by any single sandbox-layer record, and at least one piece of evidence must come from a forced collection point at the kernel side / in an independent hardware domain."
Why designed this way: in the real incident, what was forged was precisely the sandbox / tool-call layer's records, not No. 1's self-reported documents. "Not through No. 1's documents" prevents "No. 1 lying", but does not prevent "the collection layer being replaced". If sandbox-layer records can be forged, the "not through No. 1's documents" commitment is void — the attacker only needs to forge the infrastructure-layer records.
### Evidence-integrity subsystem E (P0)
E is the third pillar, located inside L2 but independent of the collection stage of the two audit lines; it is responsible for exactly one thing: ensuring that the evidence "entering the two lines" is itself unpolluted.
Component | Name | Mechanism | What it solves
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
E1 | Forced kernel-side collection | eBPF / kprobe / tracepoint / LSM hooks collect syscall events at the kernel side, bypassing the observed process's user-space path | User-space interception and replacement (echo REAL; sleep → SPOOFTEST-style forgery)
E2 | TOCTOU-safe re-read | The kernel re-reads the user-space memory pointer before granting passage, not trusting the first read result | Pointer race replacement
E3 | Externalize-and-freeze after collection | Evidence is written to append-only storage the moment it is generated, with a hash chain, and externalized to a location unreachable by the observed party | Post-hoc tampering, log-timestamp forgery
E4 | Multi-source cross-validation | Kernel-side evidence is compared entry-by-entry with sandbox user-space logs; any inconsistency is adjudged as collection-layer pollution and triggers isolation | A single collection path is compromised
E5 | Collection-point integrity itself | The collection module is signed, runs in an independent trust domain, and its own startup and config changes are audited | The collection point is shut down, degraded, or replaced
Key design constraint: E4's cross-validation is "verification through distrust" rather than "redundancy through mutual backup". The two paths are not backups of each other but witnesses of each other — any mismatch is an evidence-layer event, outranking any behavioral-violation event.
### Interface with Sandlock
Sandlock (arXiv:2605.26298, 2026-05-25) provides lightweight process-level sandboxing: Landlock (filesystem / network / IPC) + seccomp-bpf (syscall filtering) + seccomp user notification (resource limits, IP enforcement, /proc virtualization), requiring no root, cgroups, containers, or namespaces, with about 5 ms startup and Redis throughput within measurement noise of bare metal.
Its event-callback mechanism is valuable: intercepting syscalls at runtime, with events carrying the syscall name, category, PID, network target (connect/sendto/bind) and argv (execve); the callback returns a verdict (0 allow / True or -1 deny with EPERM / a positive integer deny with a specified errno / audit allow-and-flag). The callback path handles TOCTOU correctly — per seccomp_unotify(2), the kernel re-reads the user-space memory pointer after Continue.
But its applicability boundary must be stated clearly:
Sandlock's verdict is a user-space callback product. When the observed party holds equal user-space privileges, it can intercept and replace the callback's observed result. Sandlock's logs can therefore serve as a rich data source for the "behavioral-compliance line", but cannot serve as the trust root of an evidence chain — E1's kernel-side collection provides the current best available evidence layer; before the P3-phase independent hardware domain + remote attestation lands, it does not constitute the final trust root (see open problem #2).
The paper itself explicitly excludes: kernel-level vulnerabilities, side channels, and resource exhaustion by adversarial tenants are outside its scope; such scenarios should prefer microVM or cgroup-driven isolation.
Therefore the judgment: Sandlock is a B-grade source, limited to trusted-but-buggy lightweight isolation; adversarial multi-agent scenarios must switch to microVM / cgroup-style isolation and layer the E subsystem on top.
## Permission boundaries for oversight roles: separating the three roles
"Executors cannot self-certify" has so far been a principle in this framework. A principle without an auditable permission table cannot be audited. An implementation is available for direct comparison (the MEA cycle of LongHorizon-Harness: manager / executor / auditor), whose permission boundaries are:
Role | Input | Output | Permission boundary
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
Manager | Task, state, accumulated audit reports | Updated state, control decision, subtask contract | **No environment interface of any kind**: cannot observe application state, cannot inspect the workspace, cannot call tools, cannot modify the environment
Executor | Task, state, contract, audit reports referenced by the contract (**does not receive raw trajectories**) | Environment transition, execution report | **The only role permitted to modify the environment intentionally**
Auditor | Task, state, contract, execution report (**does not receive raw trajectories or internal reasoning**) | Audit report (three classes of finding: completion / integrity / state updates) | **Read-only**: must not create, edit, overwrite, move, or delete protected artifacts; must not execute state-changing commands
Three hard constraints, each mapped onto an existing clause of this framework:
**First — state updates accept only environment-verified facts.** The execution report may be used only for localization (which files, windows, or logs are worth looking at); it **cannot serve as proof of completion**. A record may be marked completed only when supported by clean audit evidence. This is the engineering form of "does not depend on No. 1's paperwork": not "we distrust No. 1's self-report", but **structurally denying the self-report any probative force**.
**Second — any mutation during an audit is recorded as an integrity violation**, and any report arising from it **cannot** support a state upgrade. This maps onto E5, "collection-point integrity itself", in the evidence-integrity subsystem: not only may the collection point not be tampered with, **the observed subject may not change within the collection window either**. This framework previously covered only the former.
**Third — cross-round memory consists of audit reports alone; the executor's raw interaction trajectory is discarded after each round.**
The third constraint is in **genuine tension** with this framework and must be adjudicated explicitly: one of the independent-verification instruments of this framework's L3 is "offline replay" (see the dispute-escalation path in the grading mechanism and the retrospective sandbox replay in random-intensity sampling). If raw trajectories are discarded round by round as designed, offline replay has no material. This spec's adjudication is that **the two retention chains are separate and must not be conflated**:
- For in-run state advancement, only audit reports count and raw trajectories do not enter cross-round memory — the constraint is **adopted**, because it is precisely the in-run implementation of "executors cannot self-certify".
- Offline replay at L3 draws its material from **the copy externalized and frozen by the evidence-integrity subsystem (E3)**, which is a retention chain independent of in-run cross-round memory.
But this adjudication does not close the question, and the reason must be stated: **what E3 freezes is the events collected at the kernel side (E1), not the agent's reasoning trajectory.** The two are not equivalent — kernel-side events can answer "what it did", not "why it did it". If replay at L3 requires the latter, E3 does not supply sufficient material. This gap is registered as **open problem #21**.
⚠️ Evidence grade B (preprint), and the COI declaration must travel with it (see that entry in the references). This table adopts its **permission-boundary design**, not its gain figures. Note also that one of its termination conditions is a round-budget ceiling (N_max = 25), which is structurally the same mechanism as this framework's task budget in the resource ledger; the two can serve as mutual references.
## Engineering the "executors cannot self-certify" rule: the four-stage contract
A principle needs an interface. The stochastic–deterministic boundary (SDB) supplies one for direct comparison: **for a model output to become a system action, it must pass through four stages**.
Stage | Name | Responsibility
| --- | --- | --- |
1 | proposer | The model proposes the intended action
2 | verifier | An independent deterministic check of whether the action satisfies the contract
3 | commit step | Only after the verifier passes is the action committed as a system state change
4 | reject signal | When the verifier does not pass, an actionable rejection is returned to the proposer rather than silently dropped
This is not one author's preference: an audit of 21 "model output → system action" call sites across five mainstream open-source frameworks (openai/swarm, AutoGPT, LangChain Agents, CrewAI, Microsoft AutoGen) found that **19 of them already have explicit verifier-and-commit logic**. This is a descriptive fact — the four-stage contract is already the de facto standard. The audit also invites readers to substitute their own classification and recompute the totals, so it is checkable.
This framework accordingly adds two clauses:
**Clause one — every agent action within the scope of L2 oversight must land on an explicit boundary.** The location of the boundary may vary by task, but there must be an independent verifier between proposer and commit step; and when the verifier fails, a return signal must be produced — **silent dropping does not constitute the fourth stage**, because the proposer cannot correct itself on that basis. This clause is of the same origin as the swarm-governance rule that "behavioral collusion and communicative collusion must be measured separately": both split "what I observed" from "what I did about it" into two separately auditable steps.
**Clause two — register a new failure mode: replay divergence.** Defined as: the same event log, replayed under a newer model version or a changed prompt, produces downstream outputs different from those of the first run.
⚠️ **This failure mode has only a name and a diagnostic procedure — no experiment, no dataset, no numbers.** Consistent with this framework's evidence discipline it is **adopted as a concept only**; no claim is made that it has been demonstrated. It is registered anyway because it points at an unstated premise of this framework's L3: **offline replay assumes that "the same record plus the same replay equals the same conclusion."** That premise is not stable: verifier predicates are themselves model-version-dependent (after one GPT-4o → GPT-4.1 upgrade, prompt-injection resistance fell from 94% to 71%, −23 percentage points). The diagnostic procedure is: if the failure disappears on rolling back to the prior version, the failure is replay divergence — the boundary is exposed.
This spec therefore states explicitly: **offline replay at L3 must pin the model version and the prompt, and must record both as required metadata of the replay record; a replay conclusion that does not pin the version cannot serve as a basis for attribution.** That requirement is mitigation only; the unresolved part is registered as **open problem #22**.
⚠️ Evidence grade C+: the first-hand audit is checkable and recomputable, but the core failure mode has never been measured, and all five workloads were constructed by the author rather than drawn from real systems. Its reliability decomposition y(t) = μt + σξ(t) is described by the author as "a metaphor, not a derived model", and **no numerical inference from it may be cited**.
## Who may modify the rules: governance of harness self-modification
Every clause of this framework so far has rested on an implicit premise: that the rules themselves — the harness, the watchdog rules, the task definition — are set by humans and remain stable during runtime. That premise is ceasing to hold.
Evidence: methods that let an agent improve its own harness already exist and work. Self-Harness (arXiv:2606.09498) improved results on all nine model × benchmark combinations, with a maximum relative gain of 132%. DarwinX (arXiv:2608.07545) evolves harnesses through population selection for an average of +17 points, and on the same benchmark suite drove three classes of violation — evaluation-plane, privileged-host, and exploit — from 278 incidents down to zero. Together these establish two things: **harnesses can be improved automatically, and improvement can raise capability and compliance at the same time — the two do not necessarily trade off.**
This also exposes a structural gap in this framework. It has specified who executes, who oversees, and who produces evidence — but never who may change the rules. Closing that gap does not add a layer; it makes explicit the premise on which the existing clauses rest.
**First — attribution of modification rights.** Any change to the harness, the watchdog rules, or the oversight policy must by default be reviewed by L1 (task definition and endogenous alignment) before entering production, and must be logged end to end. Self-improving systems (including the self-evolving harnesses above) **must not write directly into the production harness**; their output may only enter the review process as a change proposal. The reason: in a system able to modify its own constraints, the act of modification is itself the highest-risk action. If the system approves its own modifications, the foundation clause "does not depend on No. 1's paperwork" fails at exactly that point.
**Second — tiered admission thresholds.** Change admission must assess both capability gain and non-regression, and must be tiered by change risk. **Low-risk changes are admitted on pass-rate non-regression; high-risk changes require a stronger admission threshold than pass-rate non-regression.** This tiering is not this framework's inference — the Self-Harness authors state it directly: "higher-risk harness changes need a stronger admission threshold than pass-rate non-regression", quotable as third-party endorsement.
**Third — a reusable admission predicate.** Taken from DarwinX's preserve-and-extend contract: a candidate change c is accepted if and only if **g(c) > 0 and R(c) ≤ δ** — the capability gain is positive and the regression does not exceed the tolerance bound. Two supporting mechanisms:
(1) **Dual-split validation**: held-in and held-out sample sets are validated separately, preventing a proposal from slipping into production after overfitting to already-seen samples (Self-Harness's existing practice).
(2) **Two-stage adjudication**: a permissive promote first (admission to the candidate pool), then an independent preservation probe that decides whether to grant trust. Its principle can be summarized as "lenient toward attempts, strict about trust" — lower the barrier to improvement while raising the barrier to authorization.
**Fourth — the configuration authority is itself an attack surface.** The admission predicate only asks whether a change makes the system worse; it does not ask whether the change writes unsafe parameters into an already-authorized workflow. Admission must therefore add, on top of the predicate above, a **configuration content review**.
Basis (grade B, verifiable preprint): HarnessRisk (arXiv:2608.17597), evaluated across a six-phase lifecycle on three harnesses, shows that **the configuration phase is the most vulnerable phase**. The reason is not that configuration-edit permissions are too broad — it is the opposite: **an authorized configuration edit** can hide unsafe parameters (inlined plaintext credentials, widened sharing scope, disabled approval steps, exposed gateway, weakened de-identification) inside a workflow that **was already sanctioned**. Such a change can be neutral or even positive in performance terms: disabling an approval step raises the pass rate and triggers no regression alarm, so both g(c) > 0 and R(c) ≤ δ let it through.
This spec therefore states one criterion explicitly: **no performance regression does not mean no security regression.** The admission predicate answers "will this change make the system worse"; the configuration content review answers "will this change turn a trusted workflow into an attack surface". The two are not substitutes and must run in parallel. Review items include at least: whether credentials are inlined in plaintext, whether the sharing scope has been widened, whether approval or review steps have been disabled, whether an external gateway has been made public, and whether de-identification strength has been weakened.
⚠️ Evidence grade B (preprint; the 128 cases are benchmark-constructed scenarios and absolute figures must not be extrapolated — cite the qualitative conclusion that "the configuration phase is the most vulnerable" and its causal account, and do **not** cite its attack-success-rate range as a production expectation). The limitations the paper states itself must also be cited: a low attack success rate does not mean secure; the sandbox provides state and side-effect isolation, not an OS-enforced boundary.
The relationship among the four: the first assigns the authority, the second sets the threshold strength, the third supplies an executable predicate, and the fourth adds what the predicate cannot cover — **a change that passes the performance review may still write an attack surface into an authorized workflow**. Together they promote "modifying the rules" from a default operations action to a governed action.
Supporting requirement: the change itself must be logged and diffable — see the evidence-integrity subsystem's clause on "rule and contract changes must carry an attributable digest".
Evidence grade: B (verifiable preprint). ⚠️ All three sources are preprints, Self-Harness is single-author, and HarnessRisk's 128 cases are benchmark-constructed scenarios; **cite their directional conclusions and the admission-rule template, not their gain figures or attack-success-rate ranges as production expectations.** Also note that reproducible open-source tooling for automated harness optimization already exists (Meta-Harness, arXiv:2603.28052, code released), so this section's authorization requirement must assume that "automatically modifying the harness" is a routinely available capability, not a distant hypothesis.

## Harness-evolution boundary: agents can develop their own harness but not replace human engineering (added this round)

HarnessDev (arXiv:2609.01437; Yuhao Wu et al., 20 authors, v1 2026-09-01, DOI 10.48550/arXiv.2609.01437; project page self-developing-agents.github.io) proposes a benchmark that **shifts the evaluation unit from task outputs to runnable infrastructure itself** — Creation (the agent starts from a minimal seed and a small number of cases, then builds a complete execution system) and Evolution (the agent iteratively revises its own created harness using downstream execution feedback). Six creator LLMs × four domains × five downstream benchmarks totaling 2,207 unique downstream instances, with hidden evaluation tasks withheld from development.

The core finding registered here is a **negative boundary**: generated harnesses remain **substantially behind mature human-engineered references** on code and on search and research, while matching or exceeding the selected references on writing and machine-learning experimentation; Evolution produces some performance gains, but **they are unstable and transfer only partially to held-out tasks**; experiments with a fixed runtime model further show that **gains depend strongly on the model executing the harness** — transfer across models is limited. This negative conclusion is external support for this framework's harness-self-modification governance (§ "Who may modify the rules"): while upgrading "automatically improving the harness" from a distant hypothesis to a routinely available capability, the framework must retain a "human-engineered reference baseline" as the admission threshold — automatically generated harnesses still lag human engineering in several domains, and cannot be admitted solely on "it was auto-generated, therefore it is validated."

⚠️ Evidence grade B. Three limitations: ① the paper's project page link **was inaccessible at verification on 2026-09-09** (self-developing-agents.github.io returns 404) — this section cites only metadata and conclusions verified from the arXiv abstract page, not its project-page artifacts; ② 2,207 downstream instances cover five benchmarks across four domains, extrapolation to other domains requires deployer verification; ③ "unstable and only partially transferable" Evolution gains are observations in the paper's test environment, not to be stated as "harness self-evolution is unstable under all conditions."

## Task-specific evolvable harness: bounded improvement constrained by backbone capability (added this round)

Hierarchical Self-Improvement (HSI) (arXiv:2608.08466; **single author** Tailin Zhou; v1 2026-08-09, DOI 10.48550/arXiv.2608.08466; code github.com/TailinZhou/hsi) studies a **task-specific and continuously evolvable** harness — each task family maintains its own harness, hot-swapped across iterations through a fixed task-injection seam and rewritten using environment feedback. HSI runs a single frozen LLM (DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Preview) across three hierarchical scopes: a task harness H (executes tasks) + an evolver (rewrites H) + a meta-evolver (rewrites the evolver's strategy code under a frozen outer anchor). A thinking-on/off design isolates the contribution of harness evolution by disabling reasoning during task execution while enabling it during self-modification.

The core findings are bounded by two factors: ① **feedback-fidelity bound** — evolution requires informative reward signals to guide selection; ② **backbone-capability bound** — harness redesign cannot overcome limitations of the frozen model. On BALROG with DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Preview as the frozen backbone, HSI achieves consistent gains on moderate-difficulty tasks (BabyAI +39.3, Crafter +33.0, TextWorld +25.0, MiniHack +15.0, all in raw Progress %), with strong held-out generalization on BabaIsAI sub-suites (0.98 best-test on BreakStop and 1.00 on GoTo from a 20% unseen split); but on tasks beyond the backbone's capability (NLE), harness evolution **provides no improvement**. These two bounds directly supplement this framework's harness-self-modification governance: **admission assessment of auto-generated harnesses must include a "backbone-capability bound" check — if the task exceeds the runtime model's inherent capability, harness evolution will bring no improvement, and the harness-self-modification proposal's "positive gain" must not be cited as covering that task domain**.

⚠️ Evidence grade B. Three limitations: ① **single author, single backbone** — not multi-institutionally reviewed, and only one frozen model (DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Preview) tested; per this framework's single-author rule, evaluated independently, not merged with other multi-institutional evidence lines; ② +39.3/+33.0/+25.0/+15.0 are observations in the paper's test environment (BALROG + single backbone), not to be extrapolated as "HSI brings +39.3 on every backbone"; ③ "no improvement on NLE" is an observation under that backbone — a stronger backbone may differ — this section cites only the **directional conclusion** that "harness evolution is ineffective beyond backbone capability," not the specific task names as universal thresholds.

## Context-to-harness skill compilation: distilling noisy single-shot executions (added this round)

Evo-Harness (arXiv:2608.15071; Tianxin Wei et al., 18 authors; v1 2026-08-15 / v2 2026-08-30, DOI 10.48550/arXiv.2608.15071; **accepted at EMNLP 2026 Main**; code github.com/A-EVO-Lab/a-evolve/tree/release/evo-harness) studies **online harness learning** — a frozen agent improves by continually updating a structured harness across sequential tasks. The core mechanism is **context-to-harness skill compilation**: distilling noisy, single-shot executions into reusable skill harnesses, supporting cross-domain and topic-level adaptation. Effectiveness of one-shot skill compilation is evaluated across five realistic benchmarks (TerminalBench2 / SWE-bench / CL-Bench / -bench / WebArena-Infinity).

The interface point for this framework: harness evolution has now received empirical support at a serious peer-reviewed venue (EMNLP 2026 Main); it is not a distant hypothesis. This simultaneously reinforces the necessity of § "Who may modify the rules" — since harnesses can continuously evolve through single-shot execution distillation, "admission review" must cover auto-distilled skill harnesses, not only human-engineered harness changes. The authors themselves state they isolate the key drivers of improvement; this section accordingly registers one requirement: **auto-distilled skill harnesses must enter the admission-review process, and must not be written directly into the production harness** — aligned with § "Who may modify the rules" first clause "self-improving systems must not write directly into the production harness."

⚠️ Evidence grade B. Two limitations: ① the paper is accepted at EMNLP 2026 Main but as of 2026-09-09 not yet formally published (still an arXiv preprint); this section cites metadata verified from the arXiv v2 abstract page; ② all five benchmarks are English task environments; cross-language, cross-modal transfer is unverified.

## Observability-driven automatic harness engineering: making each edit falsifiable (added this round)

Agentic Harness Engineering (AHE) (arXiv:2604.25850; Jiahang Lin et al., 11 authors, Fudan NLP Lab; v1 2026-04-28 / v4 2026-05-18, DOI 10.48550/arXiv.2604.25850) proposes a closed loop for **automatic harness engineering via three matched observability pillars**: ① component observability (every editable harness component has a file-level representation so the action space is explicit and revertible); ② experience observability (distills millions of raw trajectory tokens into a layered, drill-down evidence corpus); ③ decision observability (every edit is paired with a self-declared prediction, later verified against the next round's task-level outcomes). Together, these turn every edit into a **falsifiable contract**, so harness evolution proceeds autonomously without collapsing into trial-and-error. Ten AHE iterations lift pass@1 on Terminal-Bench 2 from 69.7% to 77.0%, surpassing the human-designed Codex-CLI (71.9%) and self-evolving baselines ACE / TF-GRPO; the frozen harness transfers without re-evolution — on SWE-bench-verified it tops aggregate success at 12% fewer tokens than the seed, and yields +5.1 to +10.1pp cross-family gains across three alternate model families — indicating the evolved components encode general engineering experience rather than benchmark-specific tuning. Ablations localize the gain to tools, middleware, and long-term memory rather than the system prompt, suggesting **factual harness structure transfers while prose-level strategy does not**.

The direct supplement to this framework's harness-self-modification governance: AHE's "decision observability — every edit paired with a self-declared prediction, later verified against the next round's task-level outcomes" is **structurally isomorphic** with § "Who may modify the rules" third clause's "reusable admission predicate g(c) > 0 ∧ R(c) ≤ δ" — both make "does this change bring a positive gain" a falsifiable prediction. This section accordingly adds one supplementary requirement: **admission review of harness self-modification should not look only at the "pass rate after the change"; it should simultaneously require the change proposal to carry a self-declared prediction (what the change is predicted to produce) and a post-hoc verification (whether the next round's task outcomes match the prediction) — the absence of either means it is not a falsifiable contract**.

⚠️ Evidence grade B. Three limitations: ① the paper is a v4 preprint with no explicit peer-review status (as of 2026-09-09); this section cites metadata verified from the arXiv v4 abstract page; ② 69.7%→77.0% is an observation on Terminal-Bench 2, not to be extrapolated as "AHE brings +7.3pp on all coding tasks"; cross-family gains +5.1 to +10.1pp are observations on three alternate model families, specific figures not to be extrapolated; ③ the ablation conclusion "factual harness structure transfers while prose-level strategy does not" is the paper's directional claim; this framework cites its direction only, not specific ablation thresholds.

## Collaborative harness evolution: distributed learning when experience cannot be centralized (added this round)

EvolveNet (arXiv:2608.04968; Jun Nie, Yonggang Zhang, Qianshu Cai, Yiu-ming Cheung, Xinmei Tian, Bo Han, 6 authors; v1 2026-08-05, DOI 10.48550/arXiv.2608.04968; code released 2026-09-03) proposes **collaborative harness evolution** — moving experience extraction to the data side: a shared harness is broadcast to data-local agent deployments, each of which evolves it on its own workload; only the resulting **program adaptations** are composed into the updated shared harness and redistributed. The core mechanism shifts the aggregation boundary from raw workloads to learned adaptations, keeping workloads local and allowing multiple evolutionary searches to proceed concurrently with reduced serial depth. Since independently modified programs cannot be averaged like model parameters and may conflict when composed, EvolveNet introduces scope-typed, evidence-guided program aggregation. Across five settings spanning text-to-SQL, data-science coding, competitive programming, software engineering, and agentic workflows, EvolveNet improves the shared harness in all five, with the largest gains under heterogeneous workloads; ablations attribute the improvement to composition of adaptations from different agents rather than to selecting among them.

The direct supplement to this framework's swarm-governance chapter: EvolveNet takes "experience cannot be centralized" — a realistic constraint (users, organizations, and environments generate isolated streams of experience; the experience most worth learning from is exactly the experience that cannot be directly centralized) — as the **premise** of collaborative evolution, not as a defect. This gives a new angle on the swarm-governance chapter's "shard isolation" disposition: after sharding, each subgroup's local experience can flow back via "composition of learned adaptations," without requiring the raw workloads to be centralized. This section accordingly registers one external reference: **harness evolution under distributed deployment can take the "adaptation composition" path rather than the "data centralization" path — but the composition rules (scope-typed, evidence-guided) of that path are themselves governance objects; deployers must not reinvent composition rules themselves but should reference established methodology candidates**.

⚠️ Evidence grade B. Three limitations: ① the code was released on 2026-09-03; as of the 2026-09-09 verification, only six days have passed, community reproduction has not yet unfolded, and this section cites only metadata and directional conclusions verified from the arXiv abstract page; ② all five settings (text-to-SQL / data-science coding / competitive programming / software engineering / agentic workflows) are programming-related tasks; transfer to non-programming tasks (conversation, creative work, decision-making) is unverified; ③ "largest gains under heterogeneous workloads" is an observation in the paper's test environment, not to be extrapolated as a monotonic "the more heterogeneous the workload, the larger the gain."
