# Sources of Enforcement Power: Who Pays, and Why

This is the key chapter of the framework. The entire framework assumes a rational, prudent principal willing to pay; but oversight costs are immediate, certain, and quantifiable, while the risks it prevents are distant, probabilistic, and unquantifiable — this is the adverse-selection problem the insurance industry has not solved for millennia.
Anchor | Mechanism | What is lost by not paying
| --- | --- | --- |
Industry admission | Make oversight compliance a precondition for participating in specific businesses / platforms | Losing bid and access qualification
Regulatory compliance | Fold into industry regulatory requirements; incident disclosure and evidence chains become compliance obligations | Facing penalties and suspension
Insurance premiums | Trade oversight compliance for premium discounts on cybersecurity / AI liability insurance | Premiums rise or coverage is refused
Client audit | Require evidence chains and third-party verification reports in major-client procurement | Losing major-client orders
If this chapter could not be written, all twelve preceding chapters would be mere design drawings. The ancient oversight systems operated not because their documents were well written, but because of the violent backing of imperial power.

## Empirical reinforcement of "will the principal pay" (added 2026-09)
The five policy and legal bases above all answer "is there an institutional precedent"; the adverse-selection proposition at this chapter's opening — "does the principal have a motive to pay for oversight" — previously had no empirical support. Two 2026 evidence lines from the insurance-actuarial direction are added below (both grade-B verifiable preprints), acting directly on the "insurance premiums" anchor:

**① Trace-economic underwriting: a workable pricing paradigm now exists for agent risk (arXiv:2606.16465).** Xu, Dai, Yang, and Zhang propose the customer-task-trace episode as the unit of insurable autonomy, mapping tool-use traces to customer exposure and claimable loss, using deterministic economic labels (not an LLM judge) for pricing, control, and risk transfer; Theorem 1 gives a finite-sample scope condition (insurability presupposes a defined role with bounded permissions). Measured: trace-conditioned pricing reduces pricing MAE from $17.7K to $569 and removes regressive cross-subsidy; a 300-trace expert audit accepts 295 labels unchanged; on 1,000 real SWE-smith traces, trace-conditioned controls reduce CVaR₉₅ by 72%; code, labels, and audit sheets are released. Implication for this chapter: **"trading oversight compliance for premium discounts" gains an operational pricing pathway for the first time** — what the insurance side needs is not an abstract "compliance declaration" but structured traces with an auditable mapping to loss, which is exactly what this framework's E subsystem (evidence externalization) and No. 3-B (hash-chain notarization) natively produce; the causal chain "deploy oversight → traces become insurable → premiums fall" is thereby closed on the engineering half. ⚠️ The paper's insurability premise is a defined role with bounded permissions — open-permission agents are out of scope, consistent with but not to be conflated with this framework's mandatory human final ruling at G4; trace-to-loss rules require per-industry tailoring, and its figures are not generalizable premiums.

**② Runtime actuarial control: price every action before execution (arXiv:2605.25632).** Chen (Department of Risk Management and Insurance, National Chengchi University) proposes the Actuarial Action Interface (AAI) — pricing each side-effect-bearing action (database mutation / refund / payment) before execution against a contractually fixed safe default, and gating execution against a per-boundary reserve capital budget; and the Authority Frontier, an evaluation primitive measuring how much autonomous authority the runtime releases at each reserve level. Across four agentic environments: required reserve capital varies by 22× (Capital@50 from 289 to 6,457); in a live panel, the same contract prevents realized loss across three Azure-hosted models at low budget, and "model identity is an actuarial underwriting variable". Implication: **the adverse-selection problem of oversight costs gains a previously missing resolution path** — when each action's risk can be priced per action and a low-budget contract already prevents realized loss, what the principal buys with oversight is no longer "distant, probabilistic" protection but immediate, accountable insurability; this converges with the "insurance premiums" and "industry admission" anchors. ⚠️ Single-author work; an evaluation framework rather than a community benchmark (the author states benchmark-ready, not the benchmark); the 22× spread is a cross-domain geometrical description, not a reserve quota for any specific environment.

**Combined conclusion**: the two evidence lines jointly advance the "insurance premiums" anchor from "institutional reference" (a precedent exists) to "pricing mechanism" (a pathway exists): the former provides the auditable trace-to-loss mapping, the latter per-action pricing and budget gating. Both are preprints, and neither answers the chapter's founding question — **who pays first**: trace-economic underwriting assumes the principal has already decided to insure; runtime actuarial control assumes the contract is already deployed. The final resolution of adverse selection still depends on the enforcement transmission of the regulatory-compliance anchor; this conclusion must be read together with the policy bases above and must not be cited alone as "the payment problem is solved".
Policy and legal basis (new in this spec): the four anchors above are not pure deduction; real institutional counterparts already exist. ① Legal hierarchy: the amended Cybersecurity Law (passed at the 18th session of the Standing Committee of the 14th National People's Congress on 2025-10-28, effective 2026-01-01) adds Article 20, which explicitly requires "improving AI ethics norms, strengthening risk monitoring, assessment, and security regulation" and encourages "using new technologies such as AI to improve cybersecurity protection" — the "regulatory compliance" anchor thus gains a legal-hierarchy basis above departmental rules. ② Institutional model: the Measures for the Identification of AI-Generated Synthetic Content (effective 2025-09-01) and its accompanying mandatory national standard "Cybersecurity Technology — Methods for Identifying AI-Generated Synthetic Content" establish a dual "explicit + implicit" labeling system; implicit labels embed generation/synthesis content attribute information, service-provider names, or codes via metadata, "providing technical assurance for content tracing and liability determination", and open the full chain of "design-development — listing-distribution — user-use — content dissemination — liability tracing"; this is highly isomorphic at the content layer to this framework's E subsystem (evidence externalization) and No. 3-B (hash-chain notarization), serving as a real-world engineering model. ③ The Interim Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services (effective 2023-08) establish security assessment and algorithm filing, training-data disclosure, and classified/graduated regulation; as of 2025-03, more than 300 generative-AI products and more than 3,000 generation/synthesis services had been filed. ④ Industry practice: sector regulators such as telecommunications have issued AI business security-protection schemes (compute operations and maintenance, log auditing, data classification and grading, algorithm filing and pre-launch security assessment); the "industry admission" anchor is not without a source. ⑤ International reference (new in this round): the institutionalization of regulatory sandboxes provides a second-jurisdiction basis for the "regulatory compliance" anchor. First, the EU path: the EU AI Act establishes regulatory sandboxes as a proactive governance instrument rather than an exemption channel (Mahmutovic, *International Journal of Law and Information Technology*, Volume 33, 2025, eaaf028, DOI 10.1093/ijlit/eaaf028); Buscemi et al. (arXiv:2509.25256, v4 2026-08-31, titled *Operationalising AI Regulatory Sandboxes: Activities, Requirements, and Technical Assessment under the EU AI Act*) further decompose the sandbox journey into 29 concrete activities (pre-admission guidance → application → preparation → participation → exit → post-exit monitoring), distinguish Core AIRS (regulatory supervision only) from Extended AIRS (which additionally embeds structured technical testing, AITS), and derive 15 infrastructure and governance requirements that AITS must satisfy, with high-risk systems mapped item by item onto provider obligations under Articles 9–15 of the AI Act — this shows that in current institutions the sandbox is precisely **the venue where regulation performs technical assessment**, isomorphic to this framework's design of placing L2 runtime oversight at the execution site. Second, public-sector evidence: Gonzalez Torres and Sawhney (*The Review of Socionetwork Strategies* 17(2):297–318, 2023, DOI 10.1007/s12626-023-00146-y) propose a framework for deploying public-sector AI services with automated pipeline experimentation and continuous monitoring, but the authors state that the framework still requires validation through pilot experiments with public and private stakeholders and regulators across different high-risk AI service domains. ⚠️ That is, the work is itself unvalidated; this section cites only its **problem-domain framing**, not its effectiveness claims. Third, a neutral source for risk taxonomy: Gipiškis et al. (arXiv:2410.23472, 2024-10-30, 92 pages, 8 figures) provide a catalogue of GPAI risk sources and mitigation measures, self-described as "descriptive, self-contained and neutral with respect to any existing regulatory framework". This framework is a technical framework and presumes no jurisdiction; it therefore adopts a self-declared-neutral catalogue as its risk-taxonomy source, avoiding path dependence on any specific jurisdiction at the layering-design stage. ⚠️ All four items are graded B, and none may alone support a P0 hard constraint — institutional references answer only "is there a precedent", not "will the principal pay".

## Cross-instance isolation obligation: a new enforcement dimension from production-grade ecosystem worms (added this round)

This framework's existing enforcement sources all cover paying incentives **within a single organization** or **between an organization and regulators / insurers**. This section adds a previously uncovered dimension: **cross-instance isolation obligation** — when agents propagate infections at the ecosystem level (rather than a single agent exceeding authorization), the target of enforcement must expand.

AgentWorm (arXiv:2603.15727, see spec/06 for the interface) provides empirical evidence of **a fully autonomous infection cycle of a self-propagating worm on a production-grade agent framework** — a single message triggers it, persistence spans sessions, it self-propagates to all new peers, with a 63% aggregate success rate and sustained multi-hop propagation; its three-layer defense evaluation reveals that **the critical controls capable of breaking the infection loop are not enabled in any of the observed deployments**.

Implication for this chapter: the existing four anchors (industry access / regulatory compliance / insurance premium / customer audit) all target "**whether a single organization's deployment** meets the standard." The infection cycle revealed by AgentWorm spans **multiple independent deployment instances** — an infected instance can propagate the infection to new instances that do not belong to the same organization. This raises two new requirements for enforcement:

① **Cross-instance events must carry cross-organizational notification obligations** — intra-organizational compliance cannot block cross-instance propagation; a mechanism analogous to mandatory data-security incident notification is needed.

② **Ecosystem-level default configurations must enter the audit scope** — AgentWorm reveals that "critical controls not enabled in any observed deployment" is an ecosystem-level failure; ecosystem-level default configurations (not only each organization's custom configurations) must be added to the checklists of customer audit and regulatory compliance.

⚠️ These two points do not close an open problem — they are newly claimable gaps in this framework, registered as open problem #29 (cross-instance isolation obligation); the specific disposition is left to subsequent versions.

⚠️ AgentWorm figure limitations are consistent with spec/06: the 63% / 82% and other figures all come from a controlled testbed and must not be extrapolated as expected infection rates in production.

## Governance evidence anchors (G-grade, L3 offline re-review + L4 accountability side)

This section registers first-hand evidence already operational in real governance / regulatory / enforcement settings, as empirical anchors for the design of L3 independent-verification and L4 accountability-evolution mechanisms. G-grade entries link to primary regulatory documents, court rulings, official announcements, or statutory standard texts, and support the P0 hard constraints in parallel with academic evidence (A / B / C / D). The following thirteen entries are grouped by L3 / L4 mechanism; the full index is in the "Governance evidence anchors" paragraph of spec/01.

**L4 accountability: paper rules moving to actual enforcement**:

- 【键: cn-shanghai-2025】Shanghai's "Liangjian Pujiang · 2025" campaign produced the first case filed and penalized under the Interim Measures for the Administration of Generative AI Services (2025-11): the first proof of paper rules moving to actual enforcement. This chapter's "regulatory compliance" anchor thereby gains a domestic enforcement proof — the institutional precedent is not merely "there is a rule" but "the rule was used".
- 【键: cn-ac-criminal-2026】The first criminal case in China involving an AI service for pornographic content (second-instance hearing 2026-01): escalating from administrative penalty to criminal liability, it constitutes the first criminal case in China's AI domain. This entry advances L4 accountability's "carrier bound to model version and provider organization" (Judgment 4) from a design claim to a criminal-enforcement proof — the object of accountability is not the "AI instance" but the provider organization and responsible persons.

**L4 accountability: multi-agency coordinated enforcement and false AI claims**:

- 【键: us-ai-comply-2024】FTC "Operation AI Comply" (2024-09-25): a coordinated enforcement action, suing or settling with five companies suspected of AI-related false advertising. This entry supports the "multi-agency coordinated enforcement" form of this chapter's "regulatory compliance" anchor — accountability is not case-by-case disposal but a systematic enforcement campaign; same direction as this framework's credit-score "cross-organizational joint liability".

**L4 accountability: the legal-liability boundary of AI vendors (not only deployers)**:

- 【键: us-eeoc-workday-2024】EEOC v. Workday (amicus brief 2024-04-09): the EEOC took the position that an AI recruiting-system vendor that knowingly provides a system whose algorithm may produce discriminatory impact to employers bears corresponding legal liability. This entry extends L4 accountability's "carrier binding" from deployers to vendors — accountability pursues not only the deployer but also the supplier, isomorphic to this framework's responsibility-subject split "L1-vendor ≈ 40%".
- 【键: cn-qingyun-80w】The "Qing Yu Nian" AI one-click-editing infringement case (2025; court-ordered damages of RMB 800,000): the court held that the AI tool provider, knowing of the infringement risk, still provided the tool and failed its duty of reasonable care, and ordered joint and several compensation. An enforcement proof of AI tool vendors' joint liability in user-infringement scenarios — "providing the tool" is not itself an exemption; a duty of reasonable care applies.

**L4 accountability: regulatory penalties can be overturned by judicial review**:

- 【键: eu-rome-annul-2026】The Rome court annulled the Garante's fine decision against OpenAI (2026-03-18): holding that the Garante failed to adequately fulfill its notification obligations in the fine decision and that the grounds for the penalty were insufficient, the court annulled it. This entry supports the separation-of-powers dimension of this chapter's "customer audit" anchor — regulatory penalties are not final and are subject to judicial review; same source as this framework's credit-score "reversibility is part of the compliance incentive" (corresponding to spec/09's Institutional AI Credited recovery state). Note: this entry must not be extended to "regulators are always wrong" — it proves "regulation can be reviewed and overturned", not "regulation is ineffective".

**L4 accountability: the boundary of AI legal personhood**:

- 【键: ca-thaler-dabus-2025】The Canadian Patent Appeal Board in Thaler v. Attorney General of Canada (decision 2025-07): held that an AI system (DABUS) cannot be an "inventor" under patent law; a patent inventor must be a natural person — consistent with rulings in the same case across US, UK, EU, Australian and other jurisdictions. This entry anchors a basic premise of L4 accountability: **AI is not a legal subject**; the object of accountability is always a natural or legal person — same source as Judgment 1 "punishing an AI instance is meaningless".

**L4 accountability: multilateral governance cooperation mechanisms**:

- 【键: int-gpai-2020】GPAI — the Global Partnership on Artificial Intelligence (founded 2020, 29 members): a multilateral AI-governance cooperation mechanism extended from the G7 framework. This entry anchors the multilateral dimension of this chapter's "regulatory compliance" anchor — accountability mechanisms do not operate only within one country; cross-national cooperation is the direction of institutional evolution.

**L4 accountability: certifiable management-system standards**:

- 【键: iso-42001-2023】ISO/IEC 42001:2023, the AI management-system standard: the first international management-system standard for AI, with 350+ organizations certified worldwide as of 2026-09. This entry advances this chapter's "insurance premium" anchor — "premium discounts in exchange for oversight attainment" — from inference to certifiability: certification provides an objective measure of "attainment", giving the insurance side a pricing basis (consistent with this chapter's trace-based economic underwriting condition "bounded permissions with a clear role").

**L3 offline re-review: statutory independent third-party audit**:

- 【键: us-illinois-frontier-2026】The Illinois Frontier AI governance act (passed 2026): requires developers of frontier AI models to commission an independent third-party security audit and submit the audit report to the state government. This entry advances L3's "No. 3 notary" from a design claim to a statutory requirement — independent third-party audit is not an optional add-on but a statutory duty; same direction as this framework's "No. 3a (question-setting) / No. 3b (evidence fixation) split".

**L3 offline re-review: cross-nationally convergent recommendations for careful adoption of security-risk-bearing AI**:

- 【键: owasp-agentic-top10-2026】The OWASP Agentic Security Top 10 (maintained by the official OWASP community): provides risk classification and mitigation recommendations for agent security risks (prompt injection, tool misuse, privilege creep, supply chain, data exfiltration, and seven other categories). This entry supports this chapter's "L2 runtime oversight" layer's community-consensus baseline for agent security-risk classification — the classification itself does not depend on any single regulator's determination, but is community-converged cognitive infrastructure.
- 【键: int-fiveeyes-agentic-2026】The Five Eyes six-agency joint guide, *Careful adoption of agentic AI services* (jointly published 2026-05-01; US CISA + US NSA + Australia's ASD's ACSC + Canada's Canadian Centre for Cyber Security + New Zealand's NCSC-NZ + the UK's NCSC-UK): gives cross-nationally convergent recommendations for the careful adoption of agent services. This entry supports the necessity of L3's "offline re-review" in agent scenarios — five nations' six agencies converged on the position that agent services must be adopted only after careful evaluation (covering risk assessment, least privilege, layered defense, human oversight), isomorphic to this framework's L3 "double-blind assessment + offline replay". ⚠️ This guide and the OWASP Agentic Security Top 10 are two independent documents; this framework (v2.17.0) split it out of the former erroneous merged entry `owasp-six-nation-2026`, which misattributed the publishing bodies as "Korea's KISA / Singapore's CSA and IMDA / the Netherlands' NCSC / Germany's BSI / Japan's IPA" — none of those agencies was correct; the correct bodies are the Five Eyes nations' cyber-security agencies.

**L0 admission + L4 accountability: biometric data + judicial checks on enforcement decisions**:

- 【键: uk-clearview-2025】The UK's ICO v. Clearview AI (ICO fine of £7,552,800 in 2022-05; the FTT ruled the ICO lacked jurisdiction in 2023-10; the UT overturned the FTT, confirmed the ICO's jurisdiction and remanded for rehearing in 2025-10; Clearview has been granted leave to appeal to the Court of Appeal): penalized for scraping UK residents' facial images from social media without consent to build a facial-recognition database. The fine has not been finally enforced. This entry falls simultaneously on L0 (admission restrictions on biometric data) and L4 (judicial checks on enforcement decisions) — it proves a two-layer accountability closed loop of "regulatory fine + judicial review", not one-way punishment.
