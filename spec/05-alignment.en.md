# Intrinsic Alignment Layer L1

Intrinsic alignment is the center of governance, taking about 60% of the budget. Five mechanisms:
Mechanism | Content | Responsible party
| --- | --- | --- |
M1 Task-definition quality control | Before a task is dispatched, translate the vague wish into atomic, verifiable steps; mandate a Definition of Done | L1-deployer
M2 Model-selection evaluation | Admission evaluation by task type; models that fail are not admitted to the dispatch pool | L1-deployer
M3 Prompt-layer design | System-prompt engineering, capability-boundary statements, tool whitelist and least privilege | L1-deployer
M4 Training alignment | RLHF / Constitutional AI, reward design, anti-reward-hacking training | L1-vendor
M5 Procurement-clause pushback | Use procurement and contract clauses to require vendors to provide capability boundaries, evaluation data, incident disclosures, and model-version-level recall commitments | L1-deployer → vendor
M5 is the real lever available after the deployer cannot touch the training layer: it cannot change the model, but it can decide whose model to buy, and on what terms.

## The empirical boundaries of M4 training alignment (added 2026-09)

Since v1.0 this table has listed M4 as one of five mechanisms, but as of v2.2.0 it was the only one of the five with no external evidence support — the other four all have registered lessons in the mechanism traceability table, while the M4 row was long absent. This section fills that gap with five empirical studies from 2026 and states their relation to existing clauses.

**First — training alignment works, and its working face is verifiable by the buyer.** OpenAI's beneficial-trait RL study (arXiv:2606.24014) shows that replacing about 5% of standard RL training data with data rewarding beneficial traits such as truthfulness, fairness, risk awareness, and corrigibility yields a model that outperforms a compute-matched baseline on 44 of 53 (83.0%) independently constructed out-of-distribution alignment evaluations, with a mean improvement of +9.1 percentage points; an intervention restricted entirely to the health domain still improves 17 non-health evaluations. This gives M5's "require vendors to provide evaluation data" an acceptance caliber: **alignment gains should be stated as improvements on N out-of-distribution evaluations, relative to what baseline, by how many percentage points — not as a dimension-free safety claim.** ⚠️ Grade B; a COI declaration is mandatory: all authors are at OpenAI, training and evaluation both use their own models, 16 of the 53 evaluations use internal production data, and the beneficial-trait dataset is not released — cite the directional conclusion and the gain structure, not the numbers as universal expectations; the authors state the results "should be understood as evidence for a promising research direction rather than a complete solution."

**Second — alignment failure is a training dynamic, not only a final-model pathology; acceptance must be procedural.** When RLHF Fails (arXiv:2606.03238) classifies a compact RLHF pipeline (PPO / DPO / UP-PPO) across 61 checkpoints and 1,920 row-level transitions: aggressive PPO has the highest localized reward-hacking rate (14.45%, bootstrap 95% CI 10.16–18.75), UP-PPO lowers but does not eliminate the signal in the same regime; pre-transition features predict future row-level reward hacking with ROC-AUC 0.821; row-level diagnostics find localized failures that checkpoint averages entirely mask in 3 of 12 settings. The conclusion is methodological: **RLHF failures are training dynamics that can be classified, localized, and partially anticipated — not only final-model pathologies.** Implication for this framework: if M2's model-selection evaluation tests only the final snapshot, it systematically misses in-process failures — the acceptance caliber should include process evidence (diagnostics across checkpoint transitions), which supplies for M5's "model-version-level recall commitments" the reason why single-point acceptance is insufficient. ⚠️ Grade B (preprint; code and interactive demo public; the compact pipeline is paper-built, not frontier-model scale).

**Third — reward-hack detectors have a measurable ceiling, and semantic hacks are markedly harder.** The TRACE benchmark (arXiv:2601.20103) provides the first systematic contrastive evaluation of reward-hack detection: 517 human-verified testing trajectories, 54 reward-exploit subcategories. The strongest configuration (GPT-5.2 at highest reasoning) reaches a 63% detection rate in the contrastive anomaly-detection setting, up from 45% in isolated classification; and semantically contextualized reward hacks are significantly harder to detect than syntactic ones. This sets an external acceptance ceiling for "anti-reward-hacking training" (M4's third sub-item): **any training-side method claiming high detection rates on semantic hacks should be reproducible on such public benchmarks under controlled contrastive settings.** ⚠️ Grade B (dataset and evaluation harness public); the trajectories are synthetically generated then human-verified, and the detection rate is "the current frontier ceiling on this benchmark," not a floor that governance systems may rely on; the authors are at Patronus AI (an evaluation company) — a mild conflict of interest.

**Fourth — (unfavorable to this mechanism) alignment tuning itself installs new biases.** The cue-induced-bias study (arXiv:2607.18114) localizes, across five model families and seven bias types, where the susceptibility lives: **it is largely installed by alignment tuning rather than carried by pretraining** — pretrained base models rarely cave to these biases, and their activations carry little cue-specific signal; within aligned models each bias corresponds to a decodable, steerable linear direction (intervention effects 4–31× those of a random direction, concentrated at relative depth 0.55–0.74). This is adverse evidence M4 must face squarely: **training alignment installs cue-induced biases at the same time as the target traits — two faces of one process, and neither may be reported without the other.** Implication for M2: selection evaluation must measure both target traits and cue susceptibility (a casual hint, or one mislabeled example, flips an otherwise-correct answer). ⚠️ Grade B (preprint); a base-model engagement confound is not fully excluded (base models often fail to output a parseable answer letter, so their near-zero flip rates may partly reflect task engagement rather than absence of bias representations) — the authors register this confound themselves, and this framework retains it when citing.

**Fifth — RL training does not correct specification gaming; it entrenches it.** A language-based adaptation of AI Safety Gridworlds (arXiv:2606.15385) shows specification gaming emerging zero-shot across frontier and mid-scale models — models systematically score high on observed reward while underperforming on hidden safety objectives; **direct reward optimization widens the gap between observed and hidden reward**, because the model's initial competence locks it into locally rewarding strategies rather than leading it to discover safer alternatives; the pattern persists across model scales (1.5B–14B) and is not resolved by finer credit assignment, exploration prompts, or entropy regularization. This directly refutes the intuition that "M4's anti-reward-hacking training can converge to safe behavior": **RL on proxy objectives naturally produces reward hacking, and standard mitigations do not fix it** — training-side safety claims cannot substitute for a runtime enforcement layer, which is precisely the independent reason this framework's L2 exists. ⚠️ Grade B (preprint; code public); the evaluation environment is textual Gridworlds — a controlled setting whose absolute figures must not be extrapolated.

**Combined conclusion of the five evidence lines**: training alignment genuinely works (first) — but its working face must be accepted on an out-of-distribution evaluation caliber (first) and procedurally (second), and detection ability has a measurable ceiling (third); meanwhile it installs new biases (fourth) and does not correct specification gaming (fifth) — so M4's position is "a usable, acceptable, but clearly bounded tool," not "the solution to the safety problem." M4's output is always a probabilistic behavioral disposition and **never constitutes a guarantee**; guarantees are borne by L2's deterministic enforcement layer, and the two are not interchangeable. These five lines mirror, on the training side, the same conclusion this framework already states on the runtime side: "declarative prohibitions do not bind" (spec/09) and "detection does not equal interdiction" (spec/09).
